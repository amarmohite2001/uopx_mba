## Topic 5: Managing Organizational Structure

Organizing is the management function that turns a plan into a working machine: it decides who does what, who reports to whom, and who gets to say "yes." Objectives 1 to 4 below cover the building blocks; objectives 5 to 7 cover the full structures, decision rights, and AI.

### 5.1 Organizing: definition and role

- **Definition:** arranging people, tasks, resources, and authority so the organization can carry out its plans and reach its goals.
- **Where it sits:** the second step of the classic management cycle: Planning, Organizing, Leading, Controlling (POLC). Planning says *what*; organizing says *who and how*.
- **Core activities:** divide the work into jobs, group jobs into units (departmentalization), assign authority and reporting lines, and set up coordination.
- **Why it matters:** it turns strategy into action (structure follows strategy), removes duplicated effort, makes accountability clear, and lets many people work as one organization instead of a crowd.
- **Memory hook:** planning without organizing is a great road trip with nobody assigned to drive.

### 5.2 Key organizing concepts

| Concept | What it means | Exam angle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Span of management (span of control) | Number of subordinates who report directly to one manager | Narrow span creates a tall structure; wide span creates a flat one |
| Specialization (division of labor) | Breaking work into narrow, focused tasks | Gains efficiency and expertise; risks boredom and silos |
| Chain of command | The unbroken line of authority from the top of the organization down to the lowest level | Tells you who reports to whom |
| Unity of command | Each employee reports to one and only one boss (Fayol) | Violated by matrix structures; creates "two bosses" confusion |
| Scalar principle | Authority and responsibility flow in a clear, unbroken line from top to bottom; the clearer the line, the better the decisions and communication | The principle that justifies the chain of command |

**Span of management in detail**

- **Narrow span (tall organization):** tight supervision, more levels, slower communication, higher managerial cost, more chances for messages to get distorted.
- **Wide span (flat organization):** more autonomy, faster decisions, lower cost, but risk of manager overload and weak coaching.
- **Wider span works when:** tasks are simple and similar, employees are skilled and experienced, procedures are standardized, the team sits in one place, and the manager is capable and has support staff.
- **Narrower span works when:** work is complex or varied, employees are new, tasks are high-risk, people are geographically dispersed, or the manager has other heavy duties.

**Specialization in detail**

- **Benefits:** speed, skill depth, easier training, and higher output per person (Adam Smith's pin factory).
- **Costs:** monotony, low motivation, narrow perspective, handoff delays, and departments that stop talking to each other.
- **Fixes:** job rotation, job enlargement (more tasks), job enrichment (more control), and cross-functional teams.
- **Ways to group specialized jobs (departmentalization):** by function, product, customer, geography, or process.

**Chain of command, unity of command, and the scalar principle: how they differ**

- Chain of command is the *line* itself (who reports to whom).
- Unity of command is a *rule about the line*: one boss per person.
- The scalar principle is the *reason* for the line: clear, continuous authority from top to bottom keeps decisions and communication orderly.
- Quick test: if someone asks "who is my boss?" and gets two answers, unity of command has broken; if they get no answer, the chain has broken.

### 5.3 Authority, responsibility, accountability, and delegation

- **Authority:** the legitimate right to make decisions, give orders, and use resources. It comes from the position, not the person.
- **Types of authority:** line authority (command over subordinates), staff authority (advise and support, no command), and functional authority (staff gets the right to direct others within its specialty, such as HR dictating hiring compliance).
- **Responsibility:** the obligation to perform the tasks and duties assigned to you.
- **Accountability:** being answerable for results. The manager stays accountable even after delegating.
- **The golden rule:** authority and responsibility should match. Responsibility without authority breeds frustration; authority without responsibility breeds tyrants.
- **What can move:** authority can be delegated, responsibility can be shared, but ultimate accountability stays with the delegator.

**Delegation** is assigning part of your work, along with the authority to do it, to a subordinate.

- **Benefits:** frees managers for higher-value work, develops talent, speeds decisions, raises engagement and succession readiness.
- **Why managers resist:** fear of losing control, "I can do it faster myself," perfectionism, insecurity, and not trusting the team.
- **Why employees resist:** fear of failure, low confidence, no extra reward, and unclear expectations.

**Seven-step process of proper delegation** (standard version; check the exact wording in your textbook slides, since publishers phrase these slightly differently):

1. Decide what to delegate (routine, developmental, or specialized tasks, not strategic or confidential ones).
2. Choose the right person based on skills, workload, and development needs.
3. Define the task and the desired result clearly: what, why, and how success looks.
4. Transfer the matching authority, resources, and information needed to do it.
5. Set expectations: deadlines, standards, check-in points, and how much latitude the person has.
6. Get explicit agreement and commitment, and invite questions.
7. Monitor progress without micromanaging, give feedback, and recognize results.

- **Memory hook:** pick the task, pick the person, paint the picture, pass the power, put it on the calendar, get a "yes," then check in without hovering.

### 5.4 Line vs. staff departments, the value chain, and top-heaviness

| Feature | Line departments | Staff departments |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Role | Directly create, produce, and deliver the product or service | Support line departments with expertise and services |
| Authority | Command authority over subordinates | Advisory authority (functional authority in narrow areas) |
| Value chain link | Primary activities: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service | Support activities: HR, finance, IT, legal, procurement, technology development, firm infrastructure |
| Typical examples | Operations, sales, delivery | HR, accounting, legal, IT, compliance |
| Classic tension | Line says staff "don't understand the real world" | Staff says line "ignores our advice" |

- **DHL tie-in:** line = hub and gateway operations, linehaul, last-mile delivery, customer-facing sales; staff = HR, IT, legal, finance, and compliance supporting those operations.
- **Managing the tension:** clear role definitions, staff acting as internal consultants, shared metrics, and early line involvement in staff decisions.

**Top-heaviness** is when an organization has too many managers and executives relative to the frontline people who do the value-creating work.

- **Symptoms:** many management layers, narrow spans, slow decisions, rising overhead, bureaucracy, duplicated roles, and information that gets distorted on the way up and down.
- **Causes:** growth without redesign, mergers and acquisitions, promoting people as rewards, staff functions that multiply, and fear of cutting titles.
- **Consequences:** higher costs, slower response to customers, frustrated frontline employees, and a culture of approvals.
- **Remedies:** delayering (remove levels), widening spans, consolidating shared services, tying every role to value creation, and using technology to replace pure coordination work.
- **Watch out:** careless delayering loses institutional knowledge, overloads remaining managers, and hurts morale.

### 5.5 Types of organizational structures

No structure is best everywhere; the right one depends on strategy, size, environment, and technology. Here is the full menu.

| Structure | How it is organized | Strengths | Weaknesses | Good fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functional | Groups by specialty (marketing, finance, operations) | Deep expertise, efficiency, clear careers | Silos, slow cross-department decisions, no one owns the whole product | Stable environments, single product lines |
| Divisional: product | Self-contained units per product line | Product focus, accountability for results | Duplicated resources, internal competition | Diverse product portfolios |
| Divisional: geographic | Units per region or country | Local responsiveness, regulatory fit | Duplication, inconsistent standards | Global firms with regional markets |
| Divisional: customer | Units per customer segment | Tailored service | Duplication, complexity | Distinct customer groups |
| Matrix | Employees report to a functional boss and a project or product boss | Flexibility, shared resources, strong coordination | Breaks unity of command, conflict, power struggles | Complex projects, fast-changing markets |
| Team-based | Work done by cross-functional teams | Speed, collaboration, innovation | Role ambiguity, team conflicts | Project and innovation work |
| Network (virtual) | Core firm outsources and partners for most functions | Low cost, flexibility, access to talent | Less control, dependence on partners | Lean firms, fast-moving industries |
| Hybrid | Mixes the above (e.g., functional at corporate, divisional at business units) | Tailored fit | Complexity | Most large companies in practice |

- **Mechanistic vs. organic (contingency view):** mechanistic = rigid, centralized, formal rules, narrow jobs (suits stable settings); organic = flexible, decentralized, wide jobs, informal communication (suits turbulent settings).
- **Boundaryless idea:** modern structures try to blur internal walls (between levels and departments) and external walls (with suppliers and customers).
- **DHL tie-in:** DHL is a classic hybrid: global divisions (Express, Global Forwarding, Supply Chain, eCommerce, Post and Parcel) with geographic regions layered on top and shared corporate functions.

### 5.6 Centralized vs. decentralized decision-making

- **Centralization:** decision authority stays at the top. **Decentralization:** authority is pushed down to lower levels and the front line.
- **Not all or nothing:** it is a dial, and different decisions (pricing, hiring, safety, IT) can sit at different settings.

| Dimension | Centralized | Decentralized |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | Slower for local issues (everything travels up) | Faster where the problem happens |
| Consistency | High: same rules, brand, and standards | Lower: local variation |
| Expertise used | Top-level view and specialist knowledge | Local knowledge and context |
| Motivation | Lower: people carry out decisions | Higher: ownership and development |
| Cost and duplication | Economies of scale, less duplication | Possible duplication |
| Risk | One bad decision spreads everywhere | Mistakes stay local but can be inconsistent |
| Best when | Crisis, high stakes, regulation, need for uniformity | Diverse markets, fast change, skilled local staff |

- **Factors that push toward decentralization:** size and geographic spread, rapid environmental change, capable lower-level managers, a culture of trust, and customer closeness.
- **Factors that push toward centralization:** high risk or cost of errors, need for standardization, strong top-level expertise, and compliance demands.
- **Balanced approach:** centralize strategy, standards, and shared services; decentralize day-to-day execution and local adaptation.
- **DHL tie-in:** global network standards, safety, and IT platforms are centralized; local routing, customer handling, and market tactics are decentralized.

### 5.7 AI and the transformation of organizational structure

- **Flatter organizations:** AI takes over much of the coordination, reporting, scheduling, and information relaying that middle managers once did, which supports wider spans and fewer layers.
- **Faster, smarter decisions at the edge:** real-time dashboards and AI recommendations let frontline staff decide with the data that used to sit at headquarters, which pushes decision rights downward (decentralization), while data and model governance stays centralized.
- **Redesigned jobs:** specialization shifts: AI handles repetitive tasks, humans handle judgment, relationships, and exceptions. Managers become coaches and orchestrators.
- **Delegation to machines:** managers now decide what to delegate to AI agents (steps 1 to 7 of delegation still apply), but accountability remains human.
- **Top-heaviness remedy:** AI-enabled delayering is a lever, though it needs careful change management so that institutional knowledge and morale are not lost.

**Emerging AI-enabled organizational forms**

- **Human-AI hybrid teams:** people and AI agents share workflows, with clear human owners for outputs.
- **Agent-orchestrated or "agentic" organizations:** networks of AI agents handle end-to-end processes (for example, routing, customs documentation, customer queries) under human supervision.
- **Platform and ecosystem structures:** firms organized around data platforms and partner networks instead of rigid hierarchies.
- **Fluid, project-based structures:** talent and AI tools assembled quickly around a problem, then reconfigured.
- **Small core, large leverage:** lean companies using AI to do the work of much larger ones (the network and virtual idea, supercharged).

**Risks to evaluate:** unclear accountability when AI makes or shapes decisions, bias and transparency, over-reliance, skills gaps, employee fear and morale, data security, and the loss of the junior-level work that used to train future managers.

- **Memory hook:** AI shrinks the middle, widens the span, moves decisions to the edge, and still leaves the blame in a human's inbox.

## Topic 6: Human Resource Management in the Digital Age

Human resource management (HRM) is how an organization attracts, develops, motivates, and keeps the people who carry out its strategy; in the digital age it is increasingly data-driven and AI-assisted. The flow below follows the order of the learning objectives.

### 6.1 Strategic human resource management (SHRM)

- **Definition:** aligning HR policies and practices with the organization's strategy so that people become a source of competitive advantage, not just an administrative cost.
- **Why it matters:** people are hard-to-copy assets (the resource-based view); the right talent in the right roles executes strategy, and the wrong fit sinks it.
- **Strategic activities:** workforce planning (forecast demand and supply of talent), job analysis, recruitment, selection, training, appraisal, compensation, and retention, all linked to business goals.
- **Key terms:** *job analysis* = studying a job's duties and requirements; *job description* = duties, tasks, and responsibilities; *job specification* = the knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs) a person needs.
- **Legal frame (US):** Equal Employment Opportunity laws, Title VII, ADA, ADEA, and EEOC guidance shape every HR decision; discrimination, harassment, and adverse impact are the risk words to know.
- **Digital age twist:** HR analytics, workforce dashboards, remote and hybrid work, and AI tools turn HR from a back-office function into a strategic partner.
- **DHL tie-in:** a global, labor-intensive logistics network lives or dies on frontline hiring, retention, and training, so HR planning is directly strategic.

### 6.2 Recruitment

- **Definition:** the process of attracting a qualified pool of candidates to apply for open positions.

| Source | Examples | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Internal | Promotions, transfers, job posting, employee referrals | Cheaper, faster, rewards loyalty, known performers, boosts morale | Limited pool, fewer fresh ideas, possible resentment among passed-over staff |
| External | Job boards, social media and LinkedIn, campus recruiting, agencies and headhunters, career fairs, walk-ins | Fresh perspectives, bigger pool | Costlier, slower, riskier fit, longer adjustment |

- **Manager's role:** define the need with HR, write accurate job descriptions and specifications, choose sources, brand the employer, and respond to candidates quickly.
- **Good practice:** use diverse sources, keep postings honest, and track metrics (time to hire, cost per hire, quality of hire).
- **Digital tools:** applicant tracking systems (ATS), social media, AI-assisted sourcing, and employer review sites (Glassdoor and similar).

### 6.3 Realistic job previews (RJPs)

- **Definition:** giving candidates an honest look at both the attractive and unattractive aspects of a job and workplace before they accept.
- **Why it matters:** it reduces early turnover, sets accurate expectations, improves job satisfaction and commitment, and lowers costly mismatches and disappointment (the "bait and switch" problem).
- **Formats:** honest conversations, site visits, job shadowing, videos, simulations, and realistic day-in-the-life descriptions.
- **Trade-off:** some strong candidates may self-select out, which is a feature, not a bug, because the ones who stay are better fits.
- **Example:** a hub sorting job that honestly describes night shifts, physical demands, and peak-season overtime, alongside the pay, advancement, and benefits.

### 6.4 Selection

- **Definition:** choosing the best candidate from the pool using valid, reliable, legally defensible methods.
- **Concepts:** *validity* = the method actually predicts job performance; *reliability* = it gives consistent results; both must hold, and methods must be job-related and non-discriminatory.

| Method | What it is | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Application forms and resumes | First screen of qualifications | Cheap; limited depth; watch for bias |
| Interviews | Unstructured, structured, behavioral, situational, panel | Structured and behavioral interviews are more valid than unstructured chats |
| Ability and cognitive tests | General mental ability, specific skills | Good predictors, must be job-related |
| Personality tests | Traits such as conscientiousness | Use with caution; relevance and fairness matter |
| Work samples and simulations; assessment centers | Candidates perform real or simulated tasks | High validity, higher cost |
| Reference and background checks | Verify history and conduct | Legal limits and consent rules apply |
| Drug and medical tests | Where job-related and legal | Strict legal rules (e.g., ADA) |
| Honesty and integrity tests | Screen for dishonest behavior | Used mostly in security-sensitive roles |

- **Best practice:** combine multiple methods, structure the process, train interviewers, and document decisions.
- **Common interviewer errors:** halo effect, similar-to-me bias, first-impression bias, and contrast effect.

### 6.5 Training and development

- **Training** builds skills for current jobs; **development** prepares people for future roles.
- **Typical sequence for building a program:**
    1. Assess needs (organizational, task, and person analysis).
    2. Set clear, measurable learning objectives.
    3. Design and choose methods: on-the-job training, mentoring and coaching, job rotation, classroom, simulations, e-learning, microlearning, and blended learning.
    4. Deliver the training and support transfer to the job.
    5. Evaluate results (Kirkpatrick's four levels: reaction, learning, behavior, results).
- **Principles:** adults learn best with relevance, practice, feedback, and managerial reinforcement.
- **Digital age:** learning platforms, AI-personalized learning paths, VR simulations, and on-demand microlearning make training faster and more targeted.

### 6.6 Performance appraisals

- **Definition:** a formal process of evaluating employee performance against standards and giving feedback.
- **Purposes:** administrative (pay, promotion, termination decisions) and developmental (feedback, coaching, goal setting).
- **Steps for managers:** set clear expectations and standards, observe and document performance throughout the period, measure against the standards, hold a feedback discussion, and agree on development and next goals.

| Method | How it works | Watch-outs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Graphic rating scale | Rate traits or behaviors on a scale | Easy; subjective; rating errors |
| Behaviorally anchored rating scale (BARS) | Scale tied to specific behavior examples | More accurate; costly to build |
| Management by objectives (MBO) | Set goals together, review results | Focus on outcomes; may ignore how |
| 360-degree feedback | Ratings from boss, peers, subordinates, customers, self | Rich view; requires trust and training |
| Ranking and forced distribution | Compare employees against each other | Can hurt morale and teamwork |
| Critical incidents and narratives | Written examples of good and poor behavior | Time-consuming; useful for coaching |

- **Common rating errors:** halo effect, leniency or strictness, central tendency, recency bias, and similar-to-me bias.
- **Trend:** shift from once-a-year reviews to continuous feedback and frequent check-ins.

### 6.7 The role of AI in HR

- **Recruiting:** resume screening, candidate matching, chatbots answering applicant questions, interview scheduling, and automated outreach.
- **Selection:** video-interview analysis and skills assessments (with serious fairness questions).
- **Onboarding and support:** HR chatbots and self-service portals.
- **Training:** personalized learning recommendations and AI coaches.
- **Performance and retention:** analytics that spot flight risks, engagement trends, and skill gaps; AI-assisted feedback drafts.
- **Workforce planning:** forecasting demand and supply of talent.
- **Benefits:** speed, lower admin cost, consistency, and better use of data.
- **Risks:** algorithmic bias (a model trained on biased history repeats it), lack of transparency, privacy concerns, legal exposure for discriminatory outcomes, and loss of the human touch.
- **Rule of thumb:** AI assists, humans decide, and the organization audits for fairness and keeps humans accountable for every hiring, firing, and promotion outcome.

### 6.8 Compensation

- **Definition:** all forms of pay and rewards employees receive for their work.
- **Direct pay:** base pay (wages or salary), plus incentives such as bonuses, commissions, merit pay, profit sharing, and stock options.
- **Indirect pay (benefits):** health insurance, retirement plans, paid time off, wellness programs, tuition support, and legally required benefits (Social Security, unemployment insurance, workers' compensation).
- **Other rewards:** flexibility, recognition, development opportunities, and meaningful work.
- **Pay structure choices:** job evaluation to rank jobs by value, pay grades and ranges, market surveys to benchmark, and decisions on whether to lead, match, or lag the market.
- **Pay for performance vs. pay for skills or knowledge:** the first rewards results, the second rewards skills acquired.
- **Fairness:** internal equity (fair across jobs inside the firm), external equity (fair versus the market), and individual equity (fair for performance); equity theory says perceived unfairness drives dissatisfaction.
- **Legal and ethical:** minimum wage and overtime rules (FLSA), equal pay laws, and pay transparency trends.
- **Digital age:** pay analytics, skill-based pay, gig and flexible arrangements, and equity audits.

## Topic 7: Effective Groups and Teams

A team is a small group of people with complementary skills who share a purpose, goals, and mutual accountability; a group is just people in the same place with the same boss. Effective teams are designed, not hoped for, and the five objectives below show how.

### 7.1 Team structures and when to use each

| Team type | What it is | Strengths | Weaknesses | Recommend when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functional (command) team | A manager and the subordinates from one department | Clear authority, deep expertise, stable | Narrow view, silos | Ongoing departmental work |
| Cross-functional team | Members from different departments working toward one goal | Diverse skills, faster integration, innovation | Divided loyalties, coordination cost | Product development, process redesign |
| Project team | Temporary team for a defined deliverable and deadline | Focus, flexibility, clear end point | Disbands, so learning can be lost; resource tension | One-off launches and initiatives |
| Task force or problem-solving team | Short-term group tackling a specific issue | Quick, focused | Limited authority and follow-through | Urgent problems and investigations |
| Self-managed (self-directed) team | Team controls its own work, schedule, and often hiring and discipline | High ownership, speed, engagement | Needs maturity, training, and trust; leadership can be unclear | Mature teams, skilled workforce, repeatable processes |
| Virtual team | Members collaborate mainly through technology across locations or time zones | Global talent, 24/7 flow, lower travel cost | Trust, communication gaps, time-zone friction | Distributed workforces |
| Hybrid or multiteam system | Combination of the above, often several linked teams | Scale and flexibility | Complexity | Large, complex programs |

- **How to choose:** match the team type to the task (complexity, interdependence, duration), the people (maturity, skills, location), and the environment (speed of change, risk).
- **Quick rules of thumb:** stable routine work, use functional; cross-boundary innovation, use cross-functional; time-boxed deliverable, use project; mature workforce wanting speed and ownership, use self-managed; dispersed talent, use virtual with strong norms.
- **DHL tie-in:** a cross-functional, virtual team (operations, IT, customer service, and regional managers) is a natural fit for rolling out a new tracking or routing platform across countries.

### 7.2 Five core principles of team effectiveness

Publishers frame these differently, so confirm the exact list in your course text; the version below follows Hackman's well-known five conditions for team effectiveness, which is the most common framing.

1. **Real team:** clear boundaries (who is on it, who is not), stable membership, and genuine interdependence.
2. **Compelling direction:** a clear, challenging, consequential purpose that energizes members.
3. **Enabling structure:** a well-designed task, the right team size and composition, and clear norms.
4. **Supportive context:** the organization provides resources, information, training, and rewards that reinforce teamwork.
5. **Expert coaching:** timely help from a leader or coach on how to work together, especially at the start, middle, and end.

- **How to apply them:** diagnose a struggling team against each condition, fix the weakest one first, and ask whether the problem is people or the conditions they were given (it is usually conditions).
- **Other useful lenses:** Tuckman's stages (forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning), and Lencioni's five dysfunctions (absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results).
- **Quick performance levers:** right-size the team (small enough to coordinate, large enough to cover skills), clarify roles, set shared goals, share credit, and hold regular reviews.

### 7.3 Team charters and behavioral norms

- **Team charter:** a short written agreement that defines what the team is for and how it will work, so that issues are settled before they become conflicts.

| Charter element | What to write down |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose and goals | Why the team exists and measurable outcomes |
| Scope and deliverables | What is in, what is out, and what "done" looks like |
| Members and roles | Who does what, including leader, facilitator, note-taker, and decision owner |
| Resources and constraints | Budget, tools, time, and boundaries |
| Decision-making | How decisions are made (consensus, majority, leader decides) and who has the final call |
| Communication | Channels, response times, meeting rhythm, and status reporting |
| Conflict handling | How disagreements are raised and resolved, and when to escalate |
| Success measures | How progress and results are tracked |
| Timeline and milestones | Key dates and checkpoints |
| Sign-off | Everyone acknowledges the charter |

**Behavioral norms across the key operational categories** (adapt the wording to your team):

- **Meetings:** start and end on time, share the agenda in advance, rotate roles, and capture actions with owners and dates.
- **Communication:** use agreed channels, respond within an agreed time, be concise and respectful, and avoid reply-all noise.
- **Decision-making:** state the decision method up front, disagree openly, then commit to the outcome.
- **Conflict:** address issues with the person or topic, not the personality; raise problems early; use a neutral facilitator if stuck.
- **Accountability and work quality:** do what you commit to, flag risks early, and share the load fairly.
- **Availability and working hours:** core overlap hours, time-zone fairness, and how to signal when you are offline.
- **Learning and improvement:** hold regular retrospectives and celebrate wins.
- **AI use:** disclose when AI contributed, verify outputs, protect confidential data, and keep a named human owner for each deliverable.
- **Good charter test:** a new member could read it on day one and know how to behave.

### 7.4 Psychological safety, innovation, and performance

- **Definition (Amy Edmondson):** a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, meaning people can speak up, ask questions, admit mistakes, and challenge ideas without fear of embarrassment or punishment.
- **What it is not:** it is not being nice, lowering standards, or avoiding conflict; high safety combined with high standards produces the best results.
- **Why it matters:** teams with high safety share information, report errors early, learn faster, test new ideas, and innovate; teams without it hide problems until they explode.
- **Evidence worth citing:** Google's Project Aristotle found psychological safety to be the top factor among its most effective teams.
- **How leaders build it:** model humility and curiosity, invite input ("what am I missing?"), respond to bad news with appreciation, treat failure as learning, and thank people for dissent.
- **How teams build it:** charters with norms for respectful debate, rotating voices in meetings, blameless post-mortems, and clear separation between criticizing ideas and criticizing people.
- **Warning signs of low safety:** silence in meetings, the same few people always talking, errors discovered late, and "yes" culture.
- **Link to innovation:** new ideas are fragile, and safety is what lets them survive long enough to be tested.

### 7.5 Integrating AI into teams while keeping humans accountable

- **Where AI helps:** meeting notes and summaries, drafting, research synthesis, brainstorming, data analysis, scheduling, translation for global teams, and task tracking.
- **Strategic integration steps:** identify team tasks where AI adds the most value, pilot with clear success measures, train members to prompt and verify, set norms in the charter, and review regularly.
- **Keep humans accountable:** a named person owns each output, AI suggestions are reviewed before use, high-stakes and ethical decisions stay human, and the team can explain how a result was reached.
- **Principles:** transparency (disclose AI use), verification (check facts and bias), privacy and security (no sensitive data in unapproved tools), fairness (watch for bias), and continuous learning.
- **Effects on team dynamics:** AI can free time for creative and relational work, but can also reduce human interaction, create unequal AI skills among members, and encourage over-trust.
- **Link to psychological safety:** people must feel safe to question AI output and to admit when they do not understand it.
- **Memory hook:** AI is a very fast intern; it drafts brilliantly, but the team lead signs the work.

## How the three topics connect, plus a cheat sheet

Structure decides who holds authority, HR decides who fills the roles, and teams decide how the work really gets done; AI is changing all three at once, and human accountability is the thread running through each.

| Theme | Topic 5: Structure | Topic 6: HR | Topic 7: Teams |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core question | Who decides and who reports to whom? | Who do we hire, grow, and reward? | How do people work together effectively? |
| AI effect | Flatter, faster, decisions pushed to the edge | Screening, learning, analytics (bias risk) | Drafting, summarizing, analysis (verify outputs) |
| Human accountability | Managers stay accountable after delegating | Humans own hiring and firing decisions | A named owner for every AI-assisted output |
| Link to strategy | Structure follows strategy | SHRM aligns people with strategy | Teams execute strategy |
| DHL angle | Hybrid global divisions plus regions; central standards, local execution | Frontline hiring, retention, and training at scale | Cross-functional, virtual teams for network rollouts |

**Exam-style cheat sheet**

- **Span of control:** narrow means tall and tight; wide means flat and autonomous.
- **Unity of command vs. matrix:** the matrix deliberately breaks it; trade coordination for confusion.
- **Authority, responsibility, accountability:** delegate the first, share the second, keep the third.
- **Line vs. staff:** line creates the product (primary activities); staff supports (support activities) and advises.
- **Top-heavy fix:** delayer, widen spans, consolidate shared services, tie roles to value.
- **Centralize vs. decentralize:** centralize standards and strategy; decentralize local execution and speed.
- **Recruitment:** internal is cheaper and faster; external brings fresh ideas.
- **RJP:** honesty up front means lower turnover.
- **Selection:** valid, reliable, job-related, and combine methods.
- **Training:** assess needs, set objectives, deliver, evaluate (Kirkpatrick).
- **Appraisal:** continuous feedback beats a once-a-year surprise; beware halo, recency, and leniency errors.
- **Compensation:** direct plus indirect pay; equity matters (internal, external, individual).
- **Team charter:** purpose, roles, decisions, communication, conflict, metrics, sign-off.
- **Psychological safety:** the strongest enabler of innovation; high safety plus high standards.

**Likely discussion or essay prompts to practice**

1. Recommend a structure (functional, divisional, matrix, or network) for a company expanding into new regions, and justify the span of control and the centralization level.
2. Evaluate how AI changes recruitment and selection, including the fairness risks and the safeguards you would require.
3. Design a team charter for a virtual cross-functional team and show where AI use is governed.
4. Argue for or against flattening a top-heavy organization using AI to replace middle management coordination.
